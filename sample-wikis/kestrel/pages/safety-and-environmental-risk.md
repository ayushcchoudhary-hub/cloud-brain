# Safety and Environmental Risk: Old Ships, Thin Cover, Real Incidents

> The age profile of shadow tankers, the gap in liability insurance, the cost a spill could push onto coastal states, and two incidents often cited in this debate: the Eagle S cable damage case and the December 2024 Kerch Strait spill. Stated as reported.

Research notes from public sources, as of November 2026.

## Age profile

Shadow tankers are much older than the mainstream fleet:

- **KSE Institute (June 2026):** tankers older than 15 years made up 94% of the Russian shadow fleet.
- **KSE Institute (July 2026):** 93% of the 154 loaded shadow tankers that left Russian ports or did STS transfers were over 15 years old.
- **CREA (September 2026):** vessels at least 20 years old carried 45% of shadow tanker cargoes (54 cargoes).

For context, Frontline's CEO said in November 2025 that nearly 44% of the global VLCC fleet is older than 15 years. So age alone is not unique to the shadow fleet. CREA's concern is the combination: age, poor maintenance and inadequate insurance.

## Oversight gaps

- **Classification.** Of the 529 falsely flagged vessels the IMO identified in early 2026, 356 were not classed by any classification society (see [[how-the-shadow-fleet-operates]]).
- **Inspection history.** Lloyd's List Intelligence describes dark fleet ships as typically lacking recent inspections, poorly maintained, with unclear ownership and no insurance.
- **Liability cover.** KSE's definition of a shadow tanker is one without International Group P&I cover. Of the 48 Russian-insured shadow tankers KSE tracked in June 2026, 42 were themselves under sanctions (see [[price-cap-and-insurance]]).

## Who pays for a spill

CREA cites a report in The Times that clean-up and compensation costs from a spill by a tanker with dubious insurance could exceed EUR 1 billion for taxpayers in coastal countries. CREA does not present its own calculation. It notes that the Baltic and Arctic export routes pass through the Danish straits and the English Channel.

## Incident: Eagle S and the Estlink 2 cable (December 2024)

These facts come from court reporting and official statements.

- **What happened.** On 25 December 2024, the Estlink 2 power cable between Finland and Estonia and four internet cables were damaged in the Gulf of Finland. Prosecutors said the Cook Islands-flagged tanker Eagle S dragged its anchor, which weighed about 11,000 kg, for about 90 km across the seabed. Finland seized the ship.
- **Charges.** The Georgian captain, the Georgian first officer and the Indian second officer were charged with aggravated criminal mischief and aggravated interference with telecommunications. Prosecutors alleged gross negligence linked to the poor condition of the anchor winch. The defendants denied the charges.
- **Sanctions.** The EU listed Eagle S in its 17th package (May 2025), according to the Finnish government.
- **District court, 3 October 2025.** The Helsinki District Court dismissed the case. It found Finnish criminal law could not be applied, and that under UNCLOS the flag state or the defendants' home countries should prosecute.
- **Court of Appeal, August 2026.** The Helsinki Court of Appeal ruled that Finland does have jurisdiction, because the damage and its effects happened in Finland. It sent the case back to the district court to be heard on its merits. The decision could be appealed to the Supreme Court, with leave, by 26 October 2026. The three men had left Finland. As of early November 2026, no public report of a Supreme Court decision on leave to appeal was found.

## Incident: Kerch Strait spill (December 2024)

- On 15 December 2024, during a severe storm, the Russian-flagged tanker Volgoneft 212 sank about 5 nautical miles south of the Kerch Strait. NOAA's initial notification said it carried 4,300 tonnes of low-grade heavy fuel oil.
- A second vessel, Volgoneft 239, ran aground near Taman in the same storm.
- NOAA reported thick oil on 15 nautical miles of beachfront near Anapa.
- NOAA's record does not describe either ship as part of the shadow fleet. It is cited here as a spill example, not a sanctions case.

## What this means for coastal states

- Risk sits on the routes: the Baltic, the Danish straits, the English Channel and the Black Sea.
- The main tools are port state control checks, insurance document checks and, for ships that are stateless or sanctioned, boarding (see [[sanctions-enforcement]]).
