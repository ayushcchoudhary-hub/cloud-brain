# Espresso Dialing In

> A repeatable method for dialing in espresso at home: fix the dose, target a ratio, adjust grind by taste, and change one variable at a time.

## The three numbers

Every shot comes down to three numbers you can measure with a cheap scale and a timer:

- **Dose**: grams of dry coffee in the basket.
- **Yield**: grams of liquid espresso in the cup.
- **Time**: seconds from pump start to pump stop.

The **brew ratio** is yield divided by dose. A common starting point is 1:2, for example 18 g in and 36 g out, in roughly 25 to 30 seconds. That is a starting point, not a rule. Lighter roasts often taste better longer, around 1:2.5 to 1:3. Darker roasts often taste better shorter, around 1:1.5 to 1:2.

## Fix the dose first

Pick a dose that suits your basket and leave it alone. Most 58 mm "double" baskets are designed for 16 to 20 g. Check the number stamped on the basket or the maker's spec. Underdosing a big basket leaves too much headspace and gives a sloppy puck. Overdosing means the shower screen presses into the coffee.

## Then adjust grind by taste

Grind size is your main control. Finer grind slows the flow and raises extraction. Coarser grind speeds it up and lowers extraction.

Taste the shot, then use this map:

| Taste | Likely cause | Change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sour, sharp, thin, salty | Under-extracted | Grind finer or pull a longer ratio |
| Bitter, dry, harsh, hollow | Over-extracted | Grind coarser or pull a shorter ratio |
| Sour and bitter at once | Uneven extraction (channeling) | Fix puck prep, see below |
| Balanced but weak | Ratio too long | Pull a shorter ratio |
| Balanced but too intense | Ratio too short | Pull a longer ratio |

Time is a result, not a target. If the shot tastes great at 22 seconds, it is a great shot.

## One change at a time

Change one thing per shot and write it down. Change grind in small steps. On most home grinders, one or two clicks or a small stepless turn is enough to move time by 3 to 5 seconds. Big jumps make it hard to learn what each change did.

A simple log: date, coffee, roast date, dose, yield, time, grind setting, one word on taste. After a few bags you will know your grinder's settings for light, medium and dark roasts.

## Puck prep that matters

- **Distribution**: break up clumps with a needle tool (often called WDT). This is the single most useful prep step for most home setups.
- **Level tamp**: a level tamp matters more than tamp force. Firm and consistent is enough.
- **Dry basket**: wipe the basket before dosing.

Channeling shows up as spurts, blonding early, or a sour and bitter shot. A bottomless portafilter makes it visible.

## Freshness

Most coffees taste best for espresso from about 7 to 30 days after roasting. Very fresh beans hold a lot of gas and give foamy, erratic shots. Very old beans flow fast and taste flat, so you will need to grind finer.
