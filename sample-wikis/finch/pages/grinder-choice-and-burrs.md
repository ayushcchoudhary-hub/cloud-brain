# Grinder Choice and Burrs

> What matters when buying a home espresso grinder: burr type, adjustment steps, retention and workflow, with rough budget tiers and tradeoffs.

## The grinder matters more than the machine

A common rule in home espresso is to spend at least as much on the grinder as on the machine. A good grinder on a modest machine pulls better shots than a cheap grinder on an expensive one. Espresso needs fine, consistent grounds and very small adjustment steps. Many grinders built for filter coffee cannot do either.

## Burr types

**Conical burrs.** Common in hand grinders and many entry and mid-range electric grinders. Tend to produce a wider spread of particle sizes, which many people describe as more body and a more forgiving, rounded cup. Often run at lower speed with less heat.

**Flat burrs.** Common in mid and high-end grinders. Often produce a narrower particle spread, which many people describe as more clarity and separation of flavors, especially with light roasts. Some flat-burr grinders are less forgiving of poor puck prep.

Neither is strictly better. Burr size, alignment and geometry matter as much as type. Larger burrs (64 mm and up for flat) usually grind faster and more consistently than small ones.

## Adjustment

- **Stepped**: fixed clicks. Easy to return to a setting. Check that steps are fine enough for espresso; coarse steps leave you stuck between too fast and too slow.
- **Stepless**: a continuous collar or dial. Unlimited fine control, but harder to return to an exact setting. Mark the collar with a pen.

For espresso you want each step to change shot time by no more than a few seconds. See [[espresso-dialing-in]] for how to use those steps.

## Retention and single dosing

Retention is coffee left inside the grinder after a grind. With high retention, your next shot contains stale grounds from yesterday and your dose is off.

- Many hopper-fed grinders retain 1 g or more.
- Grinders built for single dosing aim for well under 0.5 g.
- A bellows or a few pulses after grinding helps clear retained grounds.

Single dosing (weighing beans for each shot and grinding all of them) suits people who switch coffees often. Hopper workflow suits people who drink one coffee every day and want speed.

## Budget tiers (rough)

- **Hand grinders with espresso-capable burrs.** Low cost, very good grind quality for the money, but slow and tiring for fine espresso grinding. Good for one or two shots a day.
- **Entry electric espresso grinders.** Convenient, often stepped, often with some retention and clumping. Fine for medium and dark roasts.
- **Mid-range single-dose or flat-burr grinders.** Low retention, fine adjustment, much better with light roasts. This tier is where many enthusiasts stop.
- **High-end large flat burrs.** Diminishing returns. Better consistency and speed, but the jump in the cup is smaller than the jump in price.

## Other things to check

- Noise, which varies a lot between models.
- Static and clumping. A drop of water on the beans before grinding (often called RDT) cuts static for most grinders.
- Burr replacement cost and availability.
- Footprint under kitchen cabinets.
