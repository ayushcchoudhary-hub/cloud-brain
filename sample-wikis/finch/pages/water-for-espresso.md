# Water for Espresso

> How water chemistry affects espresso taste and machine life: hardness, alkalinity, scale and corrosion, with practical targets and options for home setups.

## Why water matters twice

Water is over 90 percent of a shot, so its minerals shape taste. It also runs through a boiler and small pipes at high temperature, so its minerals decide how fast your machine scales up or corrodes. Good espresso water balances both.

## The terms

**General hardness (GH).** Mostly calcium and magnesium. These help extract flavor. Too much, and they form limescale when heated.

**Alkalinity (KH or carbonate hardness).** Mostly bicarbonate. Acts as a buffer that neutralizes acids in the coffee. High alkalinity makes coffee taste flat and chalky. Low alkalinity can make it taste sharp and sour. Alkalinity is also what drives scale formation together with calcium.

**TDS (total dissolved solids).** All dissolved minerals, usually measured in ppm. A cheap TDS pen does not tell you which minerals are present, only how much in total.

**Chloride and sulfate.** High chloride is a corrosion risk for stainless steel boilers, even when hardness is low.

## Targets

The Specialty Coffee Association water standard gives commonly cited targets for brewing water. In round numbers, expressed as calcium carbonate:

- Total hardness: around 50 to 175 ppm, with about 68 ppm as a target.
- Alkalinity: around 40 ppm, with an acceptable range up to about 75 ppm.
- TDS: around 75 to 250 ppm, with about 150 ppm as a target.
- pH: near neutral, roughly 6.5 to 7.5.
- Chlorine: none.

For espresso machines, many people aim at the low end of hardness to limit scale, since boilers concentrate minerals over time.

## Options for home

**Tap water.** Check your water supplier's published report for hardness and alkalinity. Soft tap water may be fine as is. Hard tap water will scale a machine quickly.

**Filter jugs.** Remove chlorine and some hardness, which improves taste. Results vary by cartridge age. They reduce scale risk but do not remove it.

**Bottled water.** Some low-mineral bottled waters are close to the targets. Read the label for calcium, magnesium and bicarbonate. Avoid high-sodium or very hard mineral waters.

**Mineral recipes.** Start from distilled or reverse osmosis water and add measured minerals. Published recipes, and premixed packets sold for coffee, let you hit targets exactly. This gives the most control and is popular with people who also want to protect their machine.

**Pure distilled or RO water alone.** Do not use it on its own. It tastes flat and some machines rely on minerals for water level sensors.

## Scale and descaling

- Scale shows as white crust, slower flow, longer heat-up or a hissing boiler.
- Descale on the maker's schedule using the maker's recommended product. Citric acid is common for some machines, but check, since it can damage some metals and seals.
- Softer water means less descaling. Very soft water with high chloride can corrode instead, so watch both.
